# What is algebra?

*A one-page summary of the exploration, Oct 15–19, 2026.*

## The short answer
Algebra began as a way of finding an unknown number. It became the study of **structures**: collections of things with ways of combining them, governed by rules, and studied apart from what the things are.

## How it got there

**1. The unknown as a thing in hand (al-Khwarizmi, Baghdad, c. 820).** "A square and ten roots equal thirty-nine." No symbols, only words and a picture: complete the square, read off the side, x = 3. The word *algebra* comes from *al-jabr*, "restoring."

**2. Letters change everything (Viète, 1591).** Letters stand for the *given* numbers too, not just the unknown. A whole family of problems is solved at once, and the method itself becomes an object you can study.

**3. The rules start overruling the pictures (negatives).** Symbols produce answers no picture can hold, like x = −13. A negative number is defined by what it does: −5 is what you add to 5 to get 0. "Negative times negative is positive" isn't chosen. It's *forced* if the distribution rule is to survive. The question shifts from "what is it?" to "what must it be, if the rules are to hold?"

**4. Impossible numbers, then a picture (Cardano 1545, Bombelli 1572; Wessel, Argand, Gauss c. 1800).** The cubic formula needs √−1 on the way to ordinary answers. Bombelli trusts the rules, and the impossible parts cancel. About 250 years later comes the picture: multiplying by i is a *quarter turn*, and these numbers live in a plane.

**5. Choosing the rules (Hamilton, 16 Oct 1843).** Seeking numbers for turning in 3-D space, Hamilton finds quaternions by giving up a × b = b × a, because turns in space really do depend on order. For the first time, someone *chose* which rule to drop, and the arithmetic still held together.

**6. New arithmetics everywhere (1840s–1850s).** Octonions, Boole's logic (x × x = x), Grassmann's arrows, Cayley's matrices. A matrix is a grid of numbers that moves space: same recipe, new ingredients. This is also the doorway to vector spaces.

**7. Groups: the pattern without the material.** The rule-list for moves that can be undone. The four turns of a paper square combine exactly as 1, i, −1, −i multiply: it's the same group in completely different materials. The pattern itself becomes the subject.

**8. Rings: two operations (Noether, 1920s).** The rule-list for adding and multiplying, tied together by distribution. These are the very rules that forced negative × negative = positive, now taken as the definition. Whole numbers, matrices, quaternions, clock arithmetic (where 3 × 4 = 0), and a version of Boole's logic all fit.

## The thread through it all
At each stage a new kind of number or object arrived before anyone understood it, and it was accepted because it **obeyed the rules**. Eventually the rules themselves became what algebra is about.
